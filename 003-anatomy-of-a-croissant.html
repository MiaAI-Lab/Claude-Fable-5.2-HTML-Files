Before it is breakfast, a croissant is arithmetic. One slab of butter, sealed inside one sheet of dough, folded in thirds three times: three, nine, twenty-seven. Nothing else about it is complicated. Everything else about it is difficult, and the difficulty is almost entirely temperature and patience, two things no recipe can lend you.

I have made these every week for eleven years; first badly, then acceptably, and lately well enough that I have stopped apologising at the table. What follows is my own recipe, drawn rather than listed, because lamination is easier to see than to read about.

i. The Block

Start with the butter, because the butter is in charge. In my kitchen that is 280 g of the dry, high-fat kind, beaten flat between two sheets of parchment into a square about a centimetre thick and chilled to 16 °C: cold enough to hold an edge, warm enough to bend without cracking.

Bends, does not snap

The dough, made from 500 g of flour and weighing three times what the butter does, is rolled into a larger square and turned forty-five degrees. Its four corners fold over the slab like an envelope. Pinch the seams. You now own one layer of butter and a great deal of responsibility.

ii. Three Turns

Turn one1 → 3

Roll the parcel into a long rectangle and fold it in thirds, as you would a letter to someone you respect. That is one turn: one layer of butter has become three.

Brush off the loose flour

Turn two3 → 9

Rest it in the fridge for thirty minutes, so the gluten forgives you and the butter firms up. Then turn it ninety degrees and do it again. Nine.

Seam on your right, like a book

Turn three9 → 27

Rest. Once more. Twenty-seven.

People ask why I stop at three turns. Because at a fourth the sheets grow thinner than the dough can honour, the butter smears into it, and you have made a very expensive brioche.

iii. The Roll

Roll the laminated dough to the thickness of two stacked coins and cut long triangles: 9 cm across the base, 26 cm to the point. Stretch the tip gently, then roll from wide to narrow without pressing. You are winding a spring, not strangling one.

Wide end first

Three full revolutions give the seven steps a croissant is supposed to show. Tuck the point underneath so it cannot unwind in the heat. In section, what you have made is a spiral: dough, butter, dough, butter, all the way to the middle.

Seven steps, point tucked under

iv. The Oven

Proof2 h · 26 °C

Two hours at 26 °C and no warmer; butter gives up somewhere past thirty, and a puddle is not a layer. The yeast exhales, the spiral loosens, and the croissant wobbles like a jelly when you nudge the tray. That wobble is your only timer.

The spiral loosens

Spring0–6 min · 200 °C

Then the oven. In the first six minutes the water in dough and butter flashes to steam, and every butter line becomes a tiny jack, lifting the sheet above it. The butter itself does not stay. It fries the dough on its way out and leaves a room behind. Bakers call the room an alveolus.

Butter out, steam up

Set6–12 min

By minute twelve the walls have set into membranes as thin as tracing paper, and the honeycomb holds its own weight. Do not open the door to admire it.

Half as wide again, nearly

Colour12–18 min

The last six minutes are for colour alone. Pull them at deep gold, a shade past where you lose your nerve.

v. The Shatter

Wait twenty minutes. The crumb is still setting, and you have waited this long. Then tear; do not cut.

Sound
A dry, papery crack, then a hush. If it bends quietly, it is yesterday's.
Crumb
Open, glossy cells in a spiral, like a cut cabbage. Pressed, it springs back.
Taste
Browned butter first, then wheat, then a faint, clean sourness from the long ferment.
Evidence
A lap covered in flakes. This is not a flaw in the design.
