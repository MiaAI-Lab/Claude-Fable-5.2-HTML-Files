Forty Vessels at Anchor in the RoadsPilot Cutter Reports the Roads Clearing

Ferry Strikes Buoy; No Lives LostForty Vessels Weigh; Ferry Buoy Recovered

Harbour Master’s Remarkable StatementHarbour Master’s Further Remarkable Statement

The densest fog within living memory came down upon Saint Elmo’s Bay at four o’clock yester­day after­noon, and by night­fall the busi­ness of the port was at a com­plete stand. Forty vessels lie at anchor in the Roads, their bells answer­ing one another like so many im­patient churches, and not a keel has moved above the Mole since the lamps were lit.

The mail steamer Orinthia, from Lisbon, was the last to make a berth. She was brought along­side the Vic­toria Quay by the sound of the Custom House clock and, it is said, by the smell of the kipper­ing sheds, which her pilot de­clared the surest sea-mark on the coast.

The Ferry MishapAt twenty minutes to seven the Cor­por­ation paddle ferry Marigold, cross­ing from Quarry Stairs to North­shore with 112 pas­sen­gers and the North­shore Silver Band, struck the No. 3 Bell Buoy a glan­cing blow upon the port sponson. No lives were lost. A crate of eleven hens and a euph­onium went over­board; the hens swam ashore in good order, and the euph­onium was heard to sound once, mourn­fully, as it sank.

The buoy, which broke adrift, was last heard tolling in the direc­tion of the gas­works. Mariners are warned that it is no longer to be relied upon.

Harbour Master’s StatementCaptain Josiah Pen­hallow, R.N.R., the Har­bour Master, made the fol­low­ing state­ment to our repre­sen­tative at nine o’clock:—“In one-and-forty years I have seen nothing to equal it. I held my hand before my face and could not find it; I am in­formed by the Mate that it was there through­out. This is not a local fog. It is a fog of a foreign char­acter, and I shall say so to the Com­mis­sioners.”

Asked when the port might re­open, Captain Pen­hallow replied that he would open it directly he could see it.

Latest4 a.m.—LatestMidnight.—The fog re­mains as thick as a blanket. The Signal Station reports the glass fall­ing and a breath of wind from the south-west, but can­not see its own flag­staff. Fog-guns are being fired from the Mole at half-minute inter­vals, and house­holders in Mariner’s Row are asked to bear with them.Four o’clock.—The pilot cutter Petrel reports the Roads clear­ing from the south-west before a fresh­ening breeze. The ore steamer Lady Hester weighed at 3.40, the first of the forty, and the ferry service will be re­sumed at six. The No. 3 Buoy has been found against the gas­works jetty, un­injured and still ring­ing. Captain Pen­hallow states that he can now see his hand, and is satis­fied with it.

Mayor Opens the Corporation’s New Line

Ratepayers Protest at “The Penny-Three-Farthing Folly”

The horse car is no more. At noon yester­day the Mayor, Alder­man Thomas Wickett, J.P., turned a silver key at the Pil­chard Street depot and de­clared the Cor­por­ation’s elec­tric tram­way, from the Custom House to the summit of Gallows Hill, open to the public for ever.

The line is a mile and three fur­longs in length and has cost the borough £48,600. Car No. 1, gar­landed with daf­fodils and bear­ing the Mayor, the Town Clerk and as many coun­cillors as could be accom­modated on the upper deck, set out amid cheers at six minutes past twelve.

A Hat LostThe ascent of Gallows Hill was accom­plished in four minutes, against eleven by the old horse car and seven by a man walk­ing. The only casu­alty was his Worship’s silk hat, which was lifted from his head by the trolley rope at the Rope­walk curve and carried the re­mainder of the journey upon the over­head wire, where it was much admired.

The ProtestThe pro­ceed­ings were not unani­mous. Some thirty members of the Rate­payers’ Vigi­lance League, led by Mr. Ezra Pollard, of Gallows Hill, walked ahead of the car carry­ing a banner in­scribed “A Penny-Three-Farthings on the Rates.” Mr. Pollard told our repre­sen­tative that he had climbed the hill on his own legs for sixty years and saw no reason why the borough should be taxed to carry other people up it.

He was after­wards ob­served to return to the Custom House by the 12.40 car. The fare is one penny.

Fireman and the Pillar-Box

“Sufficiently Prepaid”

At the Borough Police Court yester­day, before Mr. H. St. John Carew, Sti­pen­diary, Thomas Geake, 34, ship’s fire­man, of no fixed vessel, was charged with being drunk and dis­orderly in Packet Lane on Satur­day night.

P.C. Tregear said he found the de­fend­ant en­deav­our­ing to enter a pillar-box feet fore­most. He stated that he wished to be for­warded to Cardiff, and drew atten­tion to a stamp upon his fore­head.

The Sti­pen­diary: Was it a penny stamp?—Witness: Three-half­pence, your Worship.—The Sti­pen­diary: Then he was at least suffi­ciently pre­paid. (Laughter.)

Fined five shil­lings. The stamp was re­turned to him.

The Horse KnewWilliam Odgers, carter, sum­moned for driving a coal wagon with­out lights during the fog, said the horse knew the way. The horse was not called. Dis­missed with a caution.

A Question of HensMrs. Loveday Kitto, of Signal Terrace, sought advice con­cern­ing eleven hens which arrived upon her door­step, wet, on Monday even­ing. The Sti­pen­diary said he could not advise her to keep them and would not advise her to eat them. She should con­sult the ad­ver­tise­ment columns.

Circus Performer Breakfasts on Eleven Mackerel and a Turbot

Applauded Himself and Left by the Fish Steps

Early porters at the Fish Quay were yester­day treated to a per­form­ance for which, in the ordin­ary way, the public pays six­pence. “Admiral,” the edu­cated sea-lion of Signor Bartolo’s Grand Con­tin­ental Circus, now winter­ing at the Old Rope Walk, let him­self out of his tank on Sunday night and was not missed until he was seen, at five in the morn­ing, seated upon the marble slab of Mr. Abel Nan­carrow, fish sales­man.

By the time Mr. Nan­carrow arrived the animal had dis­posed of eleven mack­erel and a turbot, the latter valued at four­teen shil­lings. Upon the com­pletion of each fish he clapped his flip­pers and bowed to the com­pany.

P.C. Tregear, who was sent for, stated that he ap­proached the sea-lion and was offered a had­dock. He de­clined it. The animal there­upon de­scended the Fish Steps with great dignity and was lost to view in the fog.

Seen at NorthshoreLater reports, which we give with reserve, place the animal at the bathing-machines at North­shore, on the roof of the life­boat house, and in the second-class saloon of the ferry Marigold, where he is said to have tendered a mack­erel for his fare.

Signor Bartolo offers a reward of one guinea, and begs that the public will on no account applaud.

Our Serial Story

By Mrs. Aubrey Lisle, Author of “The Wrecker’s Niece.”

Chapter XIV.—What the Keeper Saw

Readers will recall that Hester Vane, having dis­covered her uncle’s ledger in the oil-store, has rowed alone to Sorrel Point to warn the keeper, old Amos Trudgeon, that the lamp is to be put out upon the night of the spring tide.

The stair wound upward into dark­ness, and with every turn the voice of the sea grew fainter and the tick of the great clock­work grew more dis­tinct, until it seemed to Hester that she climbed within the breast of some patient iron crea­ture.

Amos Trudgeon did not turn as she entered the lan­tern. He stood with one brown hand upon the brass rail, look­ing not at the lamp but through it, to the place where the Shearwater Rock should have shown its tooth above the tide.

“You’ve come about the ledger,” he said.

Hester’s heart stood still. “You know of it?”

“I’ve kep’ this light three-and-thirty year, miss. A man that watches ships learns to watch men.” He lifted the trim­ming-cloth from the rail and folded it small. “Your uncle’s lugger went out on the ebb with no lamp at her mast. She’s lying under the Point this minute. I can’t see her. But I can see the gulls that won’t settle.”

Below them, very faintly, a door closed.

The old man looked at the girl for the first time, and she saw that he was not afraid, and wished with her whole soul that he had been.

“That’ll be him now,” said Amos. “Stand you be­hind the lens, miss, where the glass is thickest—and what­ever you may hear, do not let the clock run down.”
